Two months, three hours a day: my SAT study plan

The whole plan fits in one table. The hard part is doing it every day.

5 min read

My first official SAT score was a 1280. My second was a 1600. People usually expect the explanation to be a secret technique. It isn't. It's a plan simple enough to fit in a table.

The plan

LengthTwo months
Daily timeThree hours
Practice testsTwo full-length tests every week
ReviewGoing back through my mistakes in Bluebook
ResourcesBluebook, Khan Academy, James Lu's Desmos videos on YouTube

Three hours a day for two months adds up to somewhere around 180 hours. That's the real number behind the score.

The philosophy: solve more questions

My approach was always to just solve more questions — more of the actual thing you'll face on test day.

There's a reason this works. Every question you solve is a chance to find out whether your understanding holds up under test conditions. Reading about a method feels like progress, but you don't really know a method until you've used it on a question you could have gotten wrong.

Volume only works if you review. Solving a question you got wrong and moving on teaches you nothing.

That's why the second half of the plan matters as much as the first. Two practice tests a week generate a steady supply of mistakes. Reviewing them tells you what to work on next. Step 2 covers exactly how to do that.

Why full-length tests, twice a week

A full SAT is over two hours long. Being good at individual questions is different from being good at question 22 of the second module when you're tired. Full-length practice builds that stamina, and it's the only way to find out how your timing actually holds up.

Twice a week also means you see your progress quickly. Within a couple of weeks you have enough scores to tell whether something is working.

What the resources are for

  • Bluebook is the official College Board app — the same one you take the real test in. Its practice tests are the closest thing to the real SAT that exists.
  • Khan Academy has thousands of official practice questions organized by skill. It's where you go to drill a specific weakness.
  • James Lu's Desmos videos teach you how to use the built-in graphing calculator to solve Math questions faster. More on that in the math post.

Adapting this to your schedule

Three hours a day isn't realistic for everyone, especially during a heavy semester. If you have less time, keep the ratio rather than the hours: some full-length testing every week, and real time set aside to review every test you take. Fewer tests reviewed properly beat more tests taken and forgotten.

And start earlier than you think you need to. Two months of steady work is far better than two weeks of cramming.
