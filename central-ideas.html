The four-step method for Central Ideas questions

Predict the answer before you look at the choices. It sounds slower. It isn't.

6 min read

Central Ideas and Details questions are the most straightforward questions in the Reading and Writing section. That's exactly what makes them dangerous. They feel easy, so it's tempting to skim the passage, glance at the choices, and pick whichever one sounds familiar.

The wrong answers are built to sound familiar. Every one of them borrows words from the passage. The only defense is knowing what the passage said before you let the answer choices tell you.

What these questions look like

You get a short passage — sometimes fiction, sometimes nonfiction about science, history, or the arts. Then you get one of two kinds of question:

  • Central Ideas: "Which choice best states the main idea of the text?"
  • Details: "According to the text, what is true about [something specific]?"

They share a method, but they want different things, so it's worth knowing the difference cold.

Central Ideas: the whole passage

A central idea question wants the main point. The passages are short, so this is very doable. A correct main idea does two things: it accounts for most of the important details, and it captures what the author is emphasizing.

Wrong answers almost always fail in one of three ways:

  • Too narrow. It's true, but it's about one small detail instead of the whole passage.
  • Too much. It adds information the passage never says.
  • Contradicts. It says the opposite of something in the text.

Details: one sentence

A detail question wants one specific fact, and the answer usually lives in a single sentence. The question almost always contains a keyword that points you to that sentence. Find it, and your job is just to read carefully.

The four steps

1. Restate the passage as you read. Don't skim. After each sentence, say to yourself what it just told you, in plain words. By the end you should be able to explain the passage to a friend in one breath.

2. Figure out which question it is. The main point, or one specific fact? Decide before going any further.

3. Go to the right place. For a main idea question, think about the passage as a whole — what is all of this about? For a detail question, go back to the exact sentence the keyword points to.

4. Predict, then eliminate. Answer the question in your own words before reading the choices. Then find the choice that matches your prediction. If two look close, cross out the one that adds something or contradicts the text.

Step 4 is the whole game. If you read the choices first, you're letting four strangers argue with you before you've made up your mind.

Try it: a main idea question

Read the passage and make your own prediction before you look at the choices.

Central IdeasTry it
In the 1950s, most biologists assumed the deep ocean floor was nearly lifeless: without sunlight, there seemed to be no energy source that could support an ecosystem. That assumption collapsed in 1977, when researchers exploring the Galápagos Rift found dense communities of tube worms, clams, and crabs clustered around hydrothermal vents. These animals depend on bacteria that convert chemicals from the vents into energy, a process called chemosynthesis, which allows life to thrive in complete darkness.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

Restate it: scientists thought the deep sea was lifeless because there's no sunlight → in 1977 they found thriving communities at vents → those animals get energy from chemicals instead.

Predict: life can exist without sunlight, and the vent discovery proved it. That's B.

A is too narrow, and the passage never says tube worms are the most abundant. C adds a claim the text never makes. D invents a comparison — the passage never says chemosynthesis is more efficient than anything.

Try it: a detail question

Find the keyword in the question first, then go to the sentence it points to.

DetailsTry it
Honeybees share the location of food through a movement known as the waggle dance. A forager returning to the hive moves in a figure-eight pattern, and the angle of the straight "waggle" portion relative to vertical indicates the direction of the food relative to the sun. The duration of the waggle run conveys distance: the longer the waggle lasts, the farther away the food source is.

According to the text, how does a forager bee indicate how far away a food source is?

The keyword is how far. Only one sentence talks about distance — the last one — and it says the duration of the waggle conveys it. That's C.

A is the trap. It comes from the sentence right before, which is about direction, not distance. It borrows real words from the passage, which is exactly why it feels right.

What to practice

Next time you do a practice set, cover the answer choices with your hand or a sheet of paper. Write a five-to-ten-word prediction in the margin. Only then uncover the choices.

It'll feel slow for the first dozen questions. After that, the prediction takes a few seconds — and you'll notice you stop getting pulled toward answers that merely sound right.
