Command of Evidence: turn every claim into one short sentence

If you can't state the claim in eight words, you're not ready to look at the answer choices.

8 min read

Most Reading questions ask what a passage means. Command of Evidence questions ask something different: what would prove this?

You'll be given a claim — a hypothesis, an interpretation of a poem, a conclusion drawn from a chart — and four pieces of evidence. Your job is to pick the one that supports the claim most directly. No outside knowledge needed. Everything is on the screen.

These come in two formats: textual, where the evidence is a finding or a quotation, and quantitative, where the evidence is data from a graph or table. They look different. The skill underneath is identical.

The one move that matters

Before you look at a single answer choice, compress the claim into the shortest sentence you can. I call this the test idea.

A hypothesis like "the red spot on a herring gull's beak helps chicks aim their pecks for food" becomes:

Red spot = where chicks peck.

Now you have something to hold each answer choice up against. Does this choice show chicks pecking at the red spot? Yes or no. Anything about something else goes.

Without a test idea, every choice sounds a little relevant, because every one of them mentions something from the passage. With one, three of them fall away almost instantly.

Textual evidence

Textual questions come in two flavors.

Scientific evidence. You get a hypothesis from a study or experiment. Ask yourself: if this were true, what result would I expect to see? Then pick the choice that shows that result. It's what you'd do in a science class when deciding whether an experiment supports a hypothesis.

Literary evidence. You get a claim about a poem, story, or novel, and the choices are quotations from it. You don't need to have read the work. Just ask which quotation proves this specific claim — the same thing you do when picking quotes to back up an English essay.

The method for both:

  1. Identify the claim. What exactly is being argued?
  2. Compress it into a test idea. As short as possible.
  3. Check each choice against the test idea. Off-topic? Out. Doesn't directly prove it? Out.

Try it: scientific evidence

Scientific evidenceTry it
Ethologists have proposed that the red spot on the lower beak of adult herring gulls helps newly hatched chicks aim their begging pecks, which prompt the parent to regurgitate food. To test this idea, researchers presented chicks with cardboard models of adult gull heads — some with a red spot on the beak and some without — and recorded how often the chicks pecked at each.

Which finding, if true, would most directly support the researchers' hypothesis?

Test idea: red spot = where chicks peck. A shows exactly that.

C is the trap. It's about the red spot and sounds scientific, but it measures the parents' breeding success, not where chicks aim. D would actually weaken the hypothesis. B is about time of day, which the hypothesis never mentions.

Try it: literary evidence

Build your test idea from the claim before you read the quotations.

Literary evidenceTry it

From Emily Dickinson's poem, first published in 1891:

"Hope" is the thing with feathers - That perches in the soul - And sings the tune without the words - And never stops - at all - And sweetest - in the Gale - is heard - And sore must be the storm - That could abash the little Bird That kept so many warm - I've heard it in the chillest land - And on the strangest Sea - Yet - never - in Extremity, It asked a crumb - of Me.

A student claims that the speaker presents hope as something that asks nothing of the person who has it. Which quotation from the poem most effectively illustrates this claim?

Test idea: hope asks for nothing. D says it outright — even in the worst circumstances, hope never asked for a single crumb.

B is the trap. It's a lovely line about hope never stopping, and that feels generous — but it proves persistence, not that hope asks for nothing. A describes what hope is. C shows it turns up everywhere.

Quantitative evidence

Graph and table questions look intimidating, but they come down to three skills.

Understand the claim. These questions always give you more data than you need. The paragraph is the most important part, because it tells you which numbers matter. Treat the claim as a filter.

Read the data carefully. You've seen every kind of chart in math and science class. The danger isn't the format — it's reading the wrong row, column, or unit when you're moving fast. Check the labels every time.

Judge the answer choices. Wrong answers come in exactly two types:

  • Incorrect statements. They misread the data. Easy to eliminate — just compare to the chart.
  • True but useless statements. They describe the data accurately but don't support the claim. These are the dangerous ones, because they're true. You're not looking for a true statement. You're looking for the one that proves the claim.

The process:

  1. Preview the graph or table. Title, labels, units, legend. Don't analyze yet — you don't know what you're looking for.
  2. Read the paragraph closely. If it points to something specific, like a year or a group, go straight there. If it makes a general claim, build a test idea.
  3. Eliminate the false statements. Check each choice against the data.
  4. Pick the true statement that actually proves the claim. Only one will.

Quantitative evidenceTry it
A student grew bean plants under four colors of light for 21 days and measured their average height. The student concluded that green light was the least effective of the four colors at promoting growth, both early in the experiment and by its end.

Which choice best uses data from the table to support the student's conclusion?

Test idea: green lowest, on both days. The claim has two parts — early and at the end — so the evidence has to cover both. Only B does.

D is an incorrect statement: blue (15.2 cm) beat green (9.4 cm). A and C are both true but useless, which is what makes them traps. A is about white light, not green. C describes green's growth but never compares it to anything, so it can't show green was the least effective.

What to practice

On your next practice set, write your test idea down for every Command of Evidence question before looking at the choices. Keep it under eight words. If you can't get it that short, you don't fully understand the claim yet — reread it.
